##### Background

This is a project I completed as part of my application to the United Nations.

The UN as an organization is interested in establishing egalitarian principles around the globe. It's not perfect (there were some decisions made in its establishment that benefit wealthy nations institutionally) but it is one of the most successful humanitarian organizations on the planet. One of those egalitarian umbrellas is gender equality, and so the UN seeks to achieve gender parity within each pay grade and sub-organization.

Also part of this project was projection of UURMS recruitment, but as most of my time at the UN focused on UN recruitment, I have left that to the Staff Selection page.

##### Solution

I took a couple of steps to form an initial analysis:

- Identify how UN parity has trended overall throughout the time frame represented by the data
- Aggregate across organization and pay grade to get an overview of parity at the requested level
- Create initial projections for when each UN org and pay grade would be in parity

I went a step further and applied some critical-thinking:

- Consider what may be influencing the trends in data without the data announcing it
- Consider what the data may quietly say about the future
- Source my claims with UN publications

##### Conclusion

The UN instituted a gender-equality policy many years ago. Because of the scale of the change, the average tenure of a UN employee (everyone employed at the UN spends their entire career there, unsurprisingly), and the infrequent rate at which jobs are created or open, it has taken a long time to see gender come into parity.

There is a hidden element here, however: the average age of a UN employee is very high. Almost mid-50s, in fact. This is mostly because their recruitment standards are so stringent; the _lowest-level_ professional positions require a minimum of a Master's degree _and five years' experience_. This puts the minimum age of a professional UN hire at 28, for a job whose salary is not competitive with private-sector hiring similar qualifications.

Why does this matter? Most of the women in the sample skewed younger. Looking at the rate at which employees would retire, the UN has massively overcorrected in its hiring practices. Men will retire from the UN at an integer multiple of the rate women will over the next decade. Hiring at the current rate will lead to the UN shooting right past its parity targets in the other direction.

This was the most interesting part of my project, and probably why I got hired. I was told towards the end of my internship, "The decision was between you and a woman with a report which was just as impressive. Our department currently has more women than men, so the parity directive meant you were selected." I once lost out on a Con-Ed internship the same way, so I didn't mind getting this one going my way.
